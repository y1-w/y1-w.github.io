WSE-3 Turbo / CS-4

C2 C · Spatially distributed — Distributed-memory spatial manycores

Cerebras's WSE-3 Turbo is a whole 300 mm wafer used as one processor: about 900,000 small cores, each with its own SRAM, on a 2D mesh fabric. Turbo keeps WSE-3's TSMC 5 nm silicon and 4 trillion transistors but roughly doubles clock speed; the CS-4 rack puts three of these wafers together.

02Architectural analysis

Compute organization

The wafer is patterned as a grid of die-sized regions that are stitched together across the scribe lines, so the mesh continues uninterrupted. Each core has a tensor-capable FMAC datapath, local SRAM and a router connecting to its four neighbours. Redundant cores and links route around manufacturing defects.

Turbo's gains come mainly from higher clocks: compute, SRAM bandwidth and fabric bandwidth all roughly double versus WSE-3, enabled by the CS-4 delivering about twice the power to the wafer.

Memory orchestration

All 44 GB of SRAM is distributed — about 48 KB next to each core — and there is no HBM attached to the wafer. Layers are spread spatially across regions of the wafer and activations move core-to-core over the fabric; for models larger than the wafer, weights stream in from external memory.

Interconnect & system scale

External I/O doubles to 300 GB/s per wafer. CS-4 uses the Nexus rack design (power and cooling in front, wafer 'backpacks' with I/O modules behind) and RoCEv2 networking, with an optional switchless chain topology between backpacks; I/O latency drops to 2 µs.

Programming & scheduling

The Cerebras compiler places kernels onto rectangular regions of cores and routes the fabric; the Cerebras SDK exposes per-core dataflow programming directly.

Why class C2

Hundreds of thousands of locally programmed cores with private SRAM communicating explicitly over a mesh — a distributed-memory manycore, at wafer scale.
